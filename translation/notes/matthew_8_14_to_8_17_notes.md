# 1.3 Peter’s mother-in-law (8:14–17)

Short, and nobody says a word. A healing told in four quick "and"s, then an evening of healings, then Isaiah. This unit closes the first group of three stories (1.0). Study sheet: `python3 tools/study.py 8:14-17`. Unit file: `translation/matthew/kata-matthew-8-14-17.usx` (still Greek).

**Form** is the grammar in plain words (see `docs/greek-grammar.md`). **Alternative phrases** come from four published translations, marked <sup>NASB</sup> (NASB 1995), <sup>ESV</sup>, <sup>NET</sup> (NET 2007), <sup>NIV</sup> (NIV 2011), or <sup>all</sup> when all four agree. *Italics* in an NASB phrase are its own supplied words.

## 8:14 — Into the house

> Καὶ ἐλθὼν ὁ Ἰησοῦς εἰς τὴν οἰκίαν Πέτρου εἶδεν τὴν πενθερὰν αὐτοῦ βεβλημένην καὶ πυρέσσουσαν·

**Into a house, at last.** In 8:8 the centurion said "I am not worthy that you come in under my roof", and Jesus healed from outside. The next story opens with Jesus coming *into* a house. Matthew opens the girl's story the same way, word for word: Καὶ ἐλθὼν ὁ Ἰησοῦς εἰς τὴν οἰκίαν (8:14) / καὶ ἐλθὼν ὁ Ἰησοῦς εἰς τὴν οἰκίαν τοῦ ἄρχοντος (9:23). See "Story 3 and story 7" below.

**Thrown down, the second time.** The same perfect of βάλλω as the boy (8:6) and the paralyzed man (9:2): three people in the nine stories "have been thrown down", and all three are lifted. Every translation says "lying"; we keep "thrown down" so the thread shows, as we did in 8:6.

## 8:15 — The fever left her

> καὶ ἥψατο τῆς χειρὸς αὐτῆς, καὶ ἀφῆκεν αὐτὴν ὁ πυρετός, καὶ ἠγέρθη καὶ διηκόνει αὐτῷ.

**Four "and"s.** Every clause starts with καί. Keep all four: the rush is the point (like 8:3, "and … and immediately").

**The two verbs of 4:11.** This is the biggest find in the unit.

| | who leaves | then |
|---|---|---|
| **4:11** | τότε **ἀφίησιν** αὐτὸν ὁ διάβολος — "Then the slanderer **left** him" (Tim) | καὶ ἰδοὺ ἄγγελοι προσῆλθον καὶ **διηκόνουν** αὐτῷ — "and look, messengers approached and **ministered** to him" |
| **8:15** | καὶ **ἀφῆκεν** αὐτὴν ὁ πυρετός — "and the fever **left** her" | καὶ ἠγέρθη καὶ **διηκόνει** αὐτῷ — "and she got up and **was ministering** to him" |

Same two verbs, same order, the same "X left …, … ministered to him", and both imperfect at the end (ongoing). In the wilderness the enemy leaves and messengers serve Jesus. In Petros' house the fever leaves and a woman serves him. Matthew uses διακονέω only six times: 4:11, 8:15, 20:28 (twice: "the Son of Humanity came not to be ministered to, but to minister"), 25:44, 27:55 (the women at the cross, "who followed Iesous from Galilaia, ministering to him"). Before 27:55 only messengers and this woman minister to him. **A strong ⓘ candidate** (on "left" … "was ministering", both bold).

**One more echo of the same two verbs.** In 9:5 Jesus asks which is easier, to say Ἀφίενταί σου αἱ ἁμαρτίαι ("your moral-failings are let go") or Ἔγειρε ("Get up"). That's ἀφίημι then ἐγείρω again, the order of "left her … got up". The fever is let go, and sins are let go, with one verb. Could be a second passage in the same ⓘ, or a line in its note.

**Nobody speaks.** No request, no "Master", no "trust", not even a word from Jesus. It's the only one of the nine stories with no speech at all. The page-wide colors show it: no turquoise approach, no violet plead, no dark-grey Master, no red trust. Jesus sees, and acts. Her answer isn't words either: she serves.

## 8:16 — Evening

> Ὀψίας δὲ γενομένης προσήνεγκαν αὐτῷ δαιμονιζομένους πολλούς· καὶ ἐξέβαλεν τὰ πνεύματα λόγῳ, καὶ πάντας τοὺς κακῶς ἔχοντας ἐθεράπευσεν·

**A small copy of 4:24.** Before the Sermon, Tim's 4:24: "and they brought to him all who had illness … and the demonized … and he healed them". After it, 8:16 uses the same four pieces:

| 4:24 (Tim) | 8:16 (proposed) |
|---|---|
| they brought to him (προσήνεγκαν αὐτῷ) | they brought to him |
| all who had illness (πάντας τοὺς κακῶς ἔχοντας) | all who had illness |
| the demonized (δαιμονιζομένους) | many who were demonized |
| and he healed them (ἐθεράπευσεν αὐτούς) | he healed (ἐθεράπευσεν) |

Then 9:35 repeats 4:23 almost word for word ("teaching in their synagogues and announcing the good news of the kingdom and healing every sickness and every disease"). So 4:23–24 and 9:35 frame chapters 5–9, Jesus' words (5–7) and his deeds (8–9), and 8:16 sits in the middle, echoing 4:24. Display idea: tan text on the echoed words, the color we used for 8:1 "came down from the mountain" (back to 5:1). No ⓘ needed; save that for 9:35, where the frame closes.

**Many, then all.** They bring "many"; he heals "all". The Greek puts "all" first in its clause: πάντας … ἐθεράπευσεν, "and all who had illness he healed". **Choice 7.**

## 8:17 — Isaiah

> ὅπως πληρωθῇ τὸ ῥηθὲν διὰ Ἠσαΐου τοῦ προφήτου λέγοντος· Αὐτὸς τὰς ἀσθενείας ἡμῶν ἔλαβεν καὶ τὰς νόσους ἐβάστασεν.

| Greek | Sounds like | Form | Meaning | Tim / note | Alternative phrases |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| ὅπως | HO-pohs | “so that”; the verb after it is subjunctive | so that | Tim 5:16 "so that", 2:23 "in order to". The same as ἵνα in 1:22 and 4:14 | to fulfill <sup>NASB ESV NIV</sup> · In this way <sup>NET</sup> |
| πληρωθῇ | play-roh-THAY | aorist passive subjunctive, it (-θῃ) | would be filled-full | Tim's "filled-full" (1:22, 2:15, 2:17); once "fulfilled" (4:14, the same formula) | to fulfill <sup>NASB ESV NIV</sup> · was fulfilled <sup>NET</sup> |
| τὸ ῥηθὲν | to hray-THEN | “the” + aorist passive participle, neut. (“the thing spoken”) | what was spoken | Tim 1:22, 2:15, 4:14 | what was spoken <sup>all</sup> |
| διὰ Ἠσαΐου τοῦ προφήτου | dee-A ay-sa-EE-oo too pro-FAY-too | “through” + name, genitive + “the prophet” | through Esaias the prophet | **Tim 3:3, 4:14, the same four words** | through Isaiah the prophet <sup>NASB</sup> · by the prophet Isaiah <sup>ESV</sup> · by Isaiah the prophet <sup>NET</sup> · through the prophet Isaiah <sup>NIV</sup> |

| τὰς ἀσθενείας ἡμῶν | tas as-the-NAY-as hay-MOHN | fem. noun plural, object + “of us” | our weaknesses | ἀ- "not" + σθένος "strength" ("asthenia"). The noun only here; its adjective ἀσθενής: "the flesh is weak" (26:41), "I was sick" (25:36–44). The page's OT button already says "weaknesses" | our infirmities <sup>NASB NIV</sup> · our illnesses <sup>ESV</sup> · our weaknesses <sup>NET</sup> |
| ἔλαβεν | EH-la-ben | aorist verb, he | took | λαμβάνω "take, receive": Tim 5:40 "take your shirt", 7:8 "receive" | took <sup>NASB ESV NET</sup> · took up <sup>NIV</sup> |
| καὶ τὰς νόσους | kai tas NO-soos | “and” + fem. noun plural, object (“the”, no “our”) | and the sicknesses | **Tim 4:23 "every sickness", 4:24 "sicknesses"** (νόσον, νόσοις); 9:35. The Greek has no second "our". **Choice 8** | and carried away our diseases <sup>NASB</sup> · our diseases <sup>ESV NET NIV</sup> |
| ἐβάστασεν | eh-BAS-ta-sen | aorist verb, he (augment ἐ-, -σ-) | carried | βαστάζω, "lift, carry". **Tim 3:11: John is "not worthy to carry" his sandals**, the same verb | carried away <sup>NASB</sup> · bore <sup>ESV NIV</sup> · carried <sup>NET</sup> |

**Tim's formula, word for word.** 4:14 is ἵνα πληρωθῇ τὸ ῥηθὲν διὰ Ἠσαΐου τοῦ προφήτου λέγοντος; 8:17 changes only ἵνα to ὅπως (both "so that"). Proposed: "so that what was spoken through Esaias the prophet would be filled-full, saying," which is Tim's 4:14 with his usual "filled-full" in place of his one-off "fulfilled". In 4:14 he shows the formula as colored text (cyan); do the same.

**The sixth formula.** Matthew's "so that it would be filled-full" quotes: 1:22, 2:15, 2:17, 2:23, 4:14, **8:17**, 12:17, 13:35, 21:4, 27:9. This is the only one in chapters 5–11. The one before it (4:14) and the one after it (12:17) are both Isaiah. 12:17 comes right after "and he healed them all" (12:15) and a "tell no one" (12:16), and quotes Isaiah 42, the first servant song. 8:17 quotes Isaiah 53, the last one. **Matthew's two servant-song quotes both follow a summary of healing everyone.**

**Not the Septuagint.** The Greek Old Testament of Matthew's day reads Isaiah 53:4 as "**He bears our sins** (τὰς ἁμαρτίας ἡμῶν φέρει), and is pained for us". Matthew doesn't use it. He renders the Hebrew himself ("our sicknesses he carried, our pains he bore them"), keeping the body words (checked with `tools/lxx.py isa 53:4`). The OT pop-up's note already says this. Two more Septuagint details, not yet in the pop-up:

- 53:3 LXX: "acquainted with **the bearing of sickness**" (φέρειν μαλακίαν). μαλακία is Tim's "every disease" (4:23; 9:35). The Septuagint *does* have the bodily sense, one verse earlier.
- 53:5 LXX ends "by his bruises **we were healed**" (ἰάθημεν), ἰάομαι, the light-green word of 8:8 and 8:13 ("my boy will be healed", "the boy was healed"). The verse after Matthew's quote uses the centurion's healing word.

**"Took."** λαμβάνω can be "took away" or "took upon himself". Tonight he takes the sicknesses away; the servant of Isaiah 53 takes them on. Matthew's verb leaves room for both, so keep plain "took".

## A structure to react to

```
┏ 8:14  And coming into the house of Petros,
┃       Iesous saw his mother-in-law
┃          thrown down                         ← down
┃             and burning with fever;
┃                <15> and he touched her hand,      ← the touch, in the middle
┃             and the fever left her,
┃          and she got up                      ← up
┃       ─────
┗       and was ministering to him.
┌ 8:16  Now when it became evening,                                  (light)
│       they brought to him many who were demonized;
│       ─────
│          and he threw out the spirits with a word,
│          and all who had illness he healed,
    ┌ 8:17  so that what was spoken through Esaias the prophet       (light, indented, like Tim 4:14)
    │       would be filled-full, saying,
    │       ─────
    │          “He himself, our weaknesses he took,
    └          and the sicknesses he carried.”
```

- **8:14–15 as a turn-around with indents only, no letters**: thrown down ↔ got up (down, then up), burning with fever ↔ the fever left her, and the touch in the middle. The indents here show the mirror, not grammar, so it's a new use of indents. The flat version (every clause at one indent) is the fallback.
- **8:16 ↔ 8:17**: two lines of deeds, two lines of Isaiah, both ending with the object before the verb (all who had illness he healed / our weaknesses he took / the sicknesses he carried).
- **Boxes**: a strong box for the healing; a light box for the evening; the quote in a light box indented one, as Tim lays out 4:14–16. Parallel to 8:1–4 and 8:5–13, but with no bridge box: the "coming into the house" is part of the healing.

## Choices

1. **"the house of Petros"** (pairs with 9:23 "the house of the ruler") or "Petros' house"? I lean to "the house of Petros". Word order: "And coming into the house of Petros, Iesous saw…", like Tim 2:11.
2. **πυρέσσουσαν**: "burning with fever" (shows the fire in the word), "sick with fever", or "feverish"? "Burning with fever" is my pick. It's literal, and it's still natural English.
3. **ἠγέρθη**: "she got up (ἠγέρθη got up / was raised)", Tim's "get up" with the gloss trick, or "was raised" / "rose" in the text? I lean to "got up" with the gloss: the text stays natural, the bracket shows 9:25 and 28:6, and the same treatment on 9:25 makes the pair visible. (ἐγείρω has real active forms, 8:25 ἤγειραν "they woke", so the passive could be a true passive, "was raised". All four translations read it as active: "got up", "rose".)
4. **διηκόνει**: Tim's "ministering" (keeps the 4:11 match in English, and works for 20:28 "not to be ministered to, but to minister" and 27:55) or "serving" (more everyday, but breaks the match)? Tense: "was ministering" (our imperfect rule from 8:2) or "began to minister" (what ESV, NET and NIV do: the imperfect can mean "began to")? I lean to **"was ministering to him"**: Tim's word and our rule.
5. **Ὀψίας γενομένης**: "Now when it became evening" or "Now when evening came"? I lean to "became": it's Tim's "become" for γίνομαι (4:3), and it keeps "come" for ἔρχομαι (8:14 "coming", the cyan thread of 8:5–13).
6. **δαιμονιζομένους**: "many who were demonized" (Tim's "demonized", 4:24)? "Who were" is the English shape of the participle (like "the ones following", 8:10), so no italics. Not "possessed": the Greek doesn't say it.
7. **Greek word order in 8:16b**: "and all who had illness he healed" (Greek order, puts "all" first) or "and he healed all who had illness"?
8. **8:17**: (a) Greek word order, "He himself, our weaknesses he took, and the sicknesses he carried" (like Tim's 4:16, "a great light they have seen"), or English order, "He himself took our weaknesses and carried the sicknesses"? (b) "the sicknesses" (the Greek) or "our sicknesses" (all four translations; would need *our* in italics)? I lean to Greek order and "the sicknesses".
9. **ⓘ**: one on "left … was ministering" → 4:11 (Tim's English), maybe with 9:5 (let go / get up) as a second passage or a line in the note. That's the only ⓘ I'd add here; the OT button already covers Isaiah.
10. **OT pop-up note** (`NM_QUOTES` in `tools/fetch_ot_quotes.py`): add the 53:5 LXX "we were healed (ἰάθημεν)" link to the centurion's word? And show Brenton's 53:4 "He bears our sins" as its own passage, so readers see what Matthew *didn't* quote?

## Story 3 and story 7 are a pair (8:14–15 and 9:18–26)

Last time we found story 2 ↔ story 6. This one is just as tight:

## Tidbits

### "I was weak … and you did not minister to me"

25:44 joins the two key words of this unit: "Master, when did we see you hungry or thirsty or a stranger or naked or **sick** (ἀσθενῆ) or in prison, and **did not minister** (διηκονήσαμεν) to you?" Here he carries our weaknesses (ἀσθενείας) and she ministers to him. In 25 he *is* the weak one, and the question is who ministered to him. This woman is the first person in Matthew to do what 25:31–46 asks.

### Three outsiders, then Isaiah

The leper, the Gentile, a woman. None of them could go into the inner courts of the Temple, and Jesus goes to all three first (noted in 8:1–4). The group of three ends with Isaiah's servant who takes our weaknesses: an unclean man, a foreigner, and a woman in bed with fever.

### Peter was married

Petros had a mother-in-law, so he had a wife. Paul mentions her: the other apostles and "Kephas" take believing wives along on their travels (1 Corinthians 9:5). And the house in Kapharnaoum: since early times a house there has been honored as Peter's, and a church was built over it.

### Mark tells it on a Sabbath

In Mark this happens on a Sabbath (Mark 1:21, 29–34), and "when evening came, when the sun set" (1:32) the town brings its sick: the Sabbath ended at sunset, and then they could carry them. Matthew drops the Sabbath, moves the story after the Sermon and the leper, and adds Isaiah. Luke has Jesus "rebuke" the fever (Luke 4:39), as he rebukes the wind in Matthew 8:26.

### John's words, a curiosity (maybe coincidence)

3:11, John the Baptist: "I am not **worthy** (ἱκανὸς) to **carry** (βαστάσαι) his sandals; **he himself** (αὐτὸς) will immerse you in the Holy **Spirit** (πνεύματι) and **fire** (πυρί)." Within ten verses here: "I am not worthy" (8:8), the fever, "fire" in Greek (πυρ-, 8:14–15), spirits thrown out (πνεύματα, 8:16), and "**He himself** … **carried**" (Αὐτὸς … ἐβάστασεν, 8:17). Only "not worthy" is a sure link (the 8:8 ⓘ). The rest is worth noticing, not printing.

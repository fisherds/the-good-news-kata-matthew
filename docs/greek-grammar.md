# Greek grammar, learned from our verses

A primer that grows as we translate. Every example is a word we've already worked through, so each new rule hangs on a verse you know. Not a textbook: just enough to read the **Form** column in the notes tables.

---

## The big idea: the ending does the work

English shows a word's job by **word order** ("the man touched the boy" ≠ "the boy touched the man"). Greek shows it by the **ending**, so word order is free and is used for emphasis instead. The middle of a word is the meaning; the front and back say *who, when, how*.

```
 ἐ  - καθαρίσ -  θη
 │      │        └ passive, one event   ("was …-ed")
 │      └ the meaning: clean
 └ "past" marker (the augment)
                  →  ἐκαθαρίσθη  "was made clean" (8:3)
```

## Verbs

A Greek verb packs five things into one word. Most English translations need three or four words for one Greek verb.

### 1. Aspect ("tense"): how the action is seen

This is the one that matters most, and the one English hides. Greek "tense" is mainly about the *kind* of action, and only in the indicative about *when*.

| Name | How it sees the action | Our example | Ours |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Present** | ongoing, in progress, a moving picture | λέγων (8:2), παρακαλῶν (8:5) | saying, pleading |
| **Imperfect** | ongoing *in the past* | προσεκύνει (8:2) | was bowing down (he stays down, bowing) |
| **Aorist** | one whole event, a snapshot, no comment on duration | προσελθὼν (8:2), ἐκτείνας (8:3), ἐθαύμασεν (8:10) | approaching, stretching out, he was amazed |
| **Perfect** | done, and the result still stands | βέβληται (8:6) | has been thrown down (and still lies there) |
| **Future** | will happen | θεραπεύσω (8:7), ἰαθήσεται (8:8) | I will heal, will be cured |

**Remembering "aorist"** (AIR-ist): the spelling is like "**aristocrat**" missing a few letters. The meaning comes from ἀ-όριστος, "without a horizon" (ὅρος "boundary", as in *horizon*): an action with no boundaries drawn around it, seen whole.

**Markers to spot:**

- **ἐ- at the front** (the *augment*) = past, but **only** on indicative verbs (statements). ἐ-θαύμασεν "he was amazed". Before a vowel it lengthens it instead: ἐ + ἐλθ- → **ἠ**λθ-, so προσ-ῆλθεν "he approached".
- **-σ-** before the ending = aorist or future: θεραπεύ**σ**ω "I will heal", ἐπίστευ**σ**ας "you trusted".
- **Doubling the first sound** (*reduplication*) = perfect: **βέ**βληται, from βάλλω "throw".
- **-θη-** = passive aorist ("was …-ed"): ἐκαθαρίσ**θη**, ἰά**θη** "was cured". **-θησ-** = passive future: ἰα**θήσ**εται "will be cured", ἐκβλη**θήσ**ονται "will be thrown out".

**Historical present:** a storyteller sometimes uses the present inside a past story to bring the scene close: καὶ **λέγει** αὐτῷ "and he *says* to him" (8:7). Tim renders it "said" (4:6, 4:10, 4:19), and so do we.

**Questions:** Greek can make a question with no change in the words, only tone. The oldest manuscripts have no punctuation, so editors decide. Ἐγὼ ἐλθὼν θεραπεύσω αὐτόν (8:7) is "I, coming, will heal him." or "…heal him?"; we chose the question.

**Rule we follow:** show the imperfect as "was …-ing" when it's the main verb. In 8:2 it's the one ongoing verb among aorists: the man keeps bowing; Jesus touches once.

### 2. Voice: who does it

| Voice | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Active** | the subject does it | καθαρίσαι "to make clean" |
| **Passive** | it's done to the subject | καθαρίσθητι "be (made) clean!" |
| **Middle** | the subject does it for or to itself | ἅπτομαι "touch" (8:3 ἥψατο) |

Some verbs only come in passive or middle *forms* but have active *meanings*: ἀποκριθεὶς (8:8) looks passive but means "responding". Don't translate those as passive.

### 3. Mood: what kind of statement

| Mood | What it does | Example | Ours |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Indicative** | states a fact | Θέλω (8:3) | I want |
| **Imperative** | gives a command | καθαρίσθητι (8:3), Ὕπαγε (8:4) | Be clean! Go! |
| **Subjunctive** | "maybe": after "if", "that", "so that" | ἐὰν θέλῃς (8:2) | if you want |
| **Infinitive** | "to …" | καθαρίσαι (8:2) | to make clean |
| **Participle** | an "-ing" word: a verb used like an adjective | προσελθὼν (8:2) | approaching |

**Subjunctive tip:** a long vowel in the ending (θέλ**ῃ**ς instead of θέλεις) often marks it.

### 4–5. Person and number: who

Built into the ending, so Greek doesn't need "I", "you" or "he". When it *does* add one (Ἐγὼ, 8:7), it's for emphasis.

| | Singular | Plural |
|---|---|---|
| I / we | θέλ**ω** "I want" | -ομεν "we" |
| you / you all | θέλ**εις** "you want" | -ετε "you all" |
| he, she, it / they | πορεύ**εται** "he goes" | ἥξ**ουσιν** "they will come" |

This is why "he" in "he bowed down" isn't in italics: the Greek verb already says "he".

### Participles: the "-ing" words

Matthew's favorite way to tell a story: one or two participles, then one main verb.

> καὶ **ἐκτείνας** τὴν χεῖρα **ἥψατο** αὐτοῦ **λέγων**·
> And **stretching out** the hand, he **touched** him, **saying**,

- The **main verb** (ἥψατο) is the event. The **participles** describe what goes with it.
- Participle endings look like adjective endings (they agree with their noun): -ων, -ας, -εις for a man; -ουσα for a woman (προσελθ**οῦσα**, 9:20, the woman who approaches).
- An **aorist participle** isn't "past". The "-ing" form keeps it a whole event without adding "before". Our rule: **aorist participle → "-ing"**, not "having …" (see the 8:5–13 notes).
- **Genitive absolute:** a participle and its subject both in the genitive, set at the front of a sentence → "when …". Καταβάντος δὲ αὐτοῦ "Now when he came down" (8:1); Εἰσελθόντος δὲ αὐτοῦ "Now when he entered" (8:5).

## Nouns, adjectives, pronouns

### Case: the noun's job

| Case | Job | English | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Nominative** | the subject | — | **ὁ παῖς** μου βέβληται "**my boy** has been thrown down" |
| **Genitive** | "of", whose; "from" | of, 's | **αὐτοῦ** ἡ λέπρα "**his** skin disease" (the skin disease *of him*) |
| **Dative** | "to", "for", "with", "in" | to, for | προσῆλθεν **αὐτῷ** "approached **him**" (came *to him*) |
| **Accusative** | the direct object; after some prepositions, "into" or "under" | — | ἐκτείνας **τὴν χεῖρα** "stretching out **the hand**" |
| **Vocative** | calling someone | O … ! | **Κύριε** "Master!" |

The same pronoun in four cases, all from 8:2–8:5: **αὐτοῦ** "his / of him", **αὐτῷ** "to him", **αὐτόν** "him", and as a subject it usually disappears into the verb.

### Gender

Every noun is masculine, feminine or neuter. It's **grammar, not meaning**: χείρ "hand" is feminine, λόγος "word" masculine, σκότος "darkness" neuter. Gender matters to us only when it changes the English:

- **An adjective standing alone as a person:** λεπρ**ὸς** (8:2) is masculine, so "a skin-diseased *man*".
- **The article tells you sex:** **ὁ** παῖς "the boy" (8:6). The same word with ἡ would be "the girl".
- **Plurals of people:** τοῖς ἀκολουθοῦσιν "those following" is masculine plural, which in Greek covers a mixed group.

### The article: "the"

ὁ (masc.) · ἡ (fem.) · τό (neut.), changing with case: τὴν χεῖρα, τῇ οἰκίᾳ, τοῦ ὄρους. Greek has **no word for "a"**, which is why "a" is never in italics.

Greek uses "the" in places English doesn't, and leaves it out where English wants it. **"The" with a body part or a home usually just means "his" or "my"**: ἐκτείνας τὴν χεῖρα "stretching out the hand" = his hand (Matthew can add αὐτοῦ "his", 12:49, with no change in meaning); ἐν τῇ οἰκίᾳ "in the house" = at home. So the Greek "the" is rarely emphasis. We still keep it when English can bear it: it's word for word, and it keeps matches with other passages visible (8:3 and Exodus 3:20).

### Adjectives

They match their noun in case, gender and number, so they can sit anywhere. They can also stand alone as a person or thing: λεπρὸς "a skin-diseased one", πολλοὶ "many (people)" (8:11). Adverbs often end in **-ως** like English "-ly": δειν**ῶς** "terribly" (8:6), εὐθέ**ως** "immediately" (8:3).

## Small words worth knowing

| Greek | Meaning | Note |
|---|---|---|
| καί | and | also "also, even". We keep every one |
| δέ | now, but, and | never first in its clause. We use "Now" at a new scene |
| ἀλλά | but (strong) | "not this, **but** that" (8:4, 8:8) |
| γάρ | for, because | explains (8:9) |
| ἰδού | look! | Tim's "look", never "behold" |
| ἐάν | if | takes the subjunctive (8:2) |
| ὅτι | that, because | 8:11 |
| ὡς | as, like | 8:13 |
| οὐ / μή | not | οὐ for facts, μή for wishes, commands, "if" |
